## **2\. Verification of Seed Claims**

### **Claim 1: Letter RS 18.147 orders a grain ship to Ura**

* **Finding:** Refuted (explain) \[S1, S2, S27\].  
* **Analysis:** Tablet RS 18.147 (PRU V 61; KTU 2.46) is an alphabetic cuneiform letter written in Ugaritic from Ammurapi, king of Ugarit, to the senior authority of Alashiya (Cyprus) \[S1, S27\]. It describes enemy naval raids burning cities while Ugaritic infantry and chariotry are deployed in central Hatti, and the Ugaritic fleet is stationed in the Lukka lands \[S1, S27\]. It makes zero mention of grain shipments or Ura \[S1\]. The tablet ordering an emergency maritime grain shipment to Ura is RS 20.212 (Ugaritica V 33), an Akkadian letter from the Hittite Great King commanding the administration of Ugarit to furnish a ship and crew to transport grain from Mukish to Ura because it is a "matter of life or death" \[S2, S24\].

### **Claim 2: A Hittite demand for 2,000 units (equaling 450 tons) of grain from Ugarit**

* **Finding:** Partly confirmed (explain) \[S2, S24, S32\].  
* **Analysis:** Tablet RS 20.212 does record an imperial order involving 2,000 units of grain (*ŠE.BAR*), but the grain was procured from Mukish, with Ugarit obligated to provide maritime shipping to Ura \[S2\]. The 450-metric-ton figure derives from interpreting the cuneiform sign as the major dry measure *kor* (*GUR* ![][image1] 300 liters), yielding 600,000 liters, which at bulk density 0.62–0.75 kg/L yields 372 to 450 metric tons \[S2, S24, S31\]. However, collation by Lackenbacher and recent epigraphers confirms the logogram is *BÁN* (*sūtu* ![][image1] 10 liters) \[S2, S32\]. If read as 2,000 *sūtu*, the volume is 20,000 liters, representing 12.4 to 16.2 metric tons—an amount fitting the deadweight cargo capacity of a single LBA commercial freighter (such as the 15-meter Uluburun vessel) \[S2, S18, S32\].

### **Claim 3: Tin reached the Levant by donkey caravans from Assur via Emar to Ugarit at a 15:1 buying and 7:1 selling ratio**

* **Finding:** Refuted (explain) \[S21, S23\].  
* **Analysis:** The specific 15:1 (purchase in Assur) and 7:1 (sale in Anatolia) tin-to-silver price ratios and associated donkey-caravan mechanics are exclusively attested in the Old Assyrian trade network of Kültepe/Kanesh Level II during the Middle Bronze Age (c. 1950–1750 BCE), six centuries prior \[S23\]. In the Late Bronze Age (c. 1350–1150 BCE), while Emar remained a river-caravan transshipment node, documented tin-to-silver ratios in peripheral Babylonian, Ugaritic, and Syrian texts fluctuated from 80:1 to 200:1 \[S23, S25\]. Asserting a 7:1 ratio and a 114% profit margin for LBA Ugarit is a chronological conflation \[S23\].

### **Claim 4: Tin used in LBA Mediterranean bronze came exclusively from Badakhshan (Afghanistan)**

* **Finding:** Refuted (explain) \[S21, S22\].  
* **Analysis:** There is no direct archaeological or analytical proof that Badakhshan cassiterite was mined or imported into the Eastern Mediterranean during the LBA \[S21\]. Analytical sourcing using combined tin stable isotopes (![][image2]), lead isotopes, and trace-element concentrations demonstrates multi-source procurement \[S21, S22\]. The Uluburun tin cargo originated from a combination of Central Asian deposits (Tajikistan: Mušiston; Uzbekistan: Karnab) and the southern Anatolian Taurus Mountains (Bolkardağ) \[S21\]. Simultaneously, tin ingots recovered from the Carmel coast of Israel and Mochlos on Crete match the Variscan granitic belt of Western Europe, specifically Cornwall and Devon in southwestern Britain \[S22\].

### **Claim 5: Uluburun shipwreck cargo inventory and 10:1 copper-to-tin ratio**

* **Finding:** Confirmed \[S18, S21, S29\].  
* **Analysis:** Final excavation data from the Institute of Nautical Archaeology document 354 copper oxhide ingots (four-handled and two-handled) and 121 plano-convex ("bun") and pillow-shaped ingots weighing approximately 10 metric tons \[S18\]. The tin assemblage totals approximately 1 metric ton cast into oxhide, slab, and bun forms, yielding a 10:1 alloy ratio \[S18, S21\]. The ancillary cargo includes approximately 149–150 Canaanite jars (yielding nearly 1 metric ton of terebinth resin), \~175 discoid cobalt and turquoise glass ingots, 24 logs of African blackwood/ebony, unworked elephant and hippopotamus ivory, Baltic succinite amber, luxury ceramics, and bronze weapons \[S18, S29\]. Dendrochronology and the Nefertiti gold scarab date the sinking to c. ![][image3] BCE \[S18, S29\].

### **Claim 6: Cape Gelidonya was an itinerant scrap-metal bronzesmith's vessel**

* **Finding:** Partly confirmed (explain) \[S19, S28\].  
* **Analysis:** George Bass originally hypothesized that the vessel was the floating workshop of an itinerant Syrian or Cypriot tinker based on stone anvils, hammerstones, whetstones, bun ingots, and baskets of broken bronze tools \[S19\]. However, subsequent reassessments by Muhly, Knapp, and Pulak demonstrate that 1 metric ton of metal exceeds an individual tinker's toolkit \[S19, S28\]. The vessel is now widely interpreted as a modest commercial coastal merchantman transporting recyclable scrap bronze and primary Cypriot bullion to secondary Aegean or Anatolian consumer ports following the disruption of centralized palatial distribution \[S19, S28\].

### **Claim 7: Ugaritic merchants were fluent in Akkadian, Ugaritic, Hurrian, and Cypro-Minoan**

* **Finding:** Partly confirmed (explain) \[S1, S8, S25, S30\].  
* **Analysis:** The scripts and languages are directly attested in the archives of Ugarit, but multiliteracy was institutional rather than universal among individual traders \[S8, S25\]. Major merchant-entrepreneurs like Urtenu and Rapanu operated domestic scriptoria staffed by professional scribes trained in logo-syllabic Mesopotamian cuneiform (Akkadian, Sumerian), alphabetic cuneiform (Ugaritic, Hurrian), and Cypro-Minoan (incised labels and tablets) \[S8, S25, S30\]. While merchant-officials spoke multiple vernaculars for daily commercial interaction, reading and writing four distinct scripts was maintained by specialized bureaucratic retainers \[S25, S30\].

### **Known Gaps**

* Exact names of specific LBA tin extraction localities in cuneiform inscriptions (texts mention tin arriving, but omit specific geographic toponyms).  
* Direct market retail prices for unworked timber in Ugaritic cuneiform tablets.  
* Inscribed identifying texts on Cape Gelidonya metal artifacts (heavily oxidized and devoid of surviving stamped cartouches).

### **Weakest Claims**

> 1. The identification of Ura with Gilindere/Kelenderis in Cilicia rests on geographic consensus rather than an excavated city inscription.  
> 2. The specific estimation of 5,000 swords produced from the Uluburun metal is an illustrative calculation rather than an attested historical armament delivery.  
> 3. The absolute dating of the Point Iria wreck depends entirely on ceramic stylistic synchronisms without independent dendrochronological anchoring.  
> 4. The exact division of trade profit between private merchant factors and the royal palace at Ugarit cannot be mathematically fixed across surviving archives.  
> 5. Whether the 12 hippopotamus teeth on Uluburun originated from the Nile Delta or coastal Levantine river swamps remains chemically indistinguishable.  
> 6. The degree to which arsenical copper was deliberately re-introduced during the 12th century BCE versus representing unsegregated remelted scrap.  
> 7. The absolute duration of the multi-year drought across each individual micro-region during the 1200–1150 BCE transition.  
> 8. The exact route taken by Baltic amber before entering Mycenaean maritime supply lines (Danubian vs. Atlantic fluvial networks).  
> 9. The literacy level of individual traveling Levantine ship captains in writing Cypro-Minoan without scribal retainers.  
> 10. The reading of *BÁN* versus *GUR* in tablet RS 20.212, while confirmed by recent collations, remains an active point of divergence in popular histories.

### **Anachronism Check**

* The Old Assyrian commercial network at Kanesh (Level II, c. 1950–1750 BCE) was explicitly separated from the Late Bronze Age discussion; its 15:1 Assur purchase and 7:1 Anatolian sales ratios were verified and marked as anachronistic when applied to the LBA.  
* Middle Bronze Age tin-to-silver price data were excluded from Late Bronze Age economic calculations, ensuring that all analyzed ratios (80:1 to 200:1) belong strictly to late-second-millennium archives (Ugarit, Emar, Deir el-Medina, Middle Assyria).

### **Confidence Summary**

* **Verification of Seed Claims:** High confidence. Primary text editions and isotopic sourcing studies directly refute or clarify all seven seed claims.  
* **Metrology and Commodity Specifications:** High confidence. Rooted directly in published cuneiform editions (PRU, KTU, Ugaritica V), Linear B corpora (Docs²), and excavation reports (Uluburun, Cape Gelidonya, Point Iria).  
* **Palatial Economics and Institutional Concepts:** High confidence. Grounded in consensus models of Late Bronze Age political economy and primary administrative records.  
* **Historiographical Disputes:** High confidence. Competing scholarly factions, lead analytical arguments, and evidential grounds are thoroughly documented without artificial resolution.
